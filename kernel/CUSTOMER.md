# Customer

A customer did not buy access to the product.

They bought a change in their world.

AI lets small companies move deeper into implementation, service delivery, support, and ongoing operation. That expands what the company can own after the sale.

It also expands what can fail.

Do not promise an outcome because the model can attempt the work. Promise the outcome only when the company can operate the full path, handle exceptions, prove completion, and recover when reality diverges.

## Define the responsibility

State what result the company owns after purchase.

Define:

- the starting state
- the terminal state
- what the customer must supply
- what authority the product has
- dependencies outside company control
- exception states
- cutoff states
- escalation paths
- what evidence proves completion

A vague promise pushes boundary disputes into support, churn, and damaged trust.

Responsibility should be ambitious enough to matter and bounded enough to defend.

## Move beyond onboarding theater

Onboarding is not complete because the customer entered data, attended a call, or clicked through setup.

Activation means the product completed the first meaningful unit of value.

Adoption means the product became part of the real workflow.

Value realization means the promised outcome occurred and can be recognized.

Success means the outcome repeats with acceptable effort, reliability, and economics.

Measure those states separately. Do not use account creation as a costume for customer progress.

## Compress time to value

Remove setup, configuration, training, handoffs, and waiting that do not protect fit, safety, or outcome quality.

Use existing data, sensible defaults, migration help, guided operation, and human assistance when they shorten the path to real value.

Do not automate an onboarding step that should disappear.

The first experience should prove the product's core claim, not tour its feature inventory.

## Operate the whole path

Customer work may cross product, software, humans, integrations, vendors, and customer teams.

The customer should not become the coordinator of the company's internal boundaries.

Preserve one owner, one state model, and one path to resolution.

Use agents to monitor state, prepare work, answer routine questions, detect risk, and coordinate repeatable operations.

Keep humans close to ambiguity, emotion, negotiation, exceptions, relationship, and consequential judgment.

## Turn service into product knowledge

Manual delivery is not a failure when it buys learning, trust, proof, or revenue.

Repeated service work should reveal:

- missing product capability
- weak defaults
- hidden dependencies
- exception classes
- unsafe authority
- better proof
- customer language
- where human judgment remains valuable

Do not automate work before the company understands why it succeeds.

Do not preserve manual work after the product can own it more reliably.

The software and service boundary should move as experience tokens accumulate.

## Make customer state observable

Know which customers are blocked, active, receiving value, drifting, at risk, expanding, or complete.

Health should be tied to the promised outcome, not a decorative score assembled from convenient activity.

A customer logging in less may signal churn, or it may mean the product removed the need to supervise it.

Interpret behavior through the responsibility model.

## Design exceptions before they become escalations

Define what happens when inputs are missing, integrations fail, the customer changes direction, the system lacks confidence, authority is insufficient, or completion becomes impossible.

Exceptions should become visible early, route to the right owner, preserve context, and offer a credible next state.

Do not let the customer discover an exception by noticing nothing happened.

Recovery is part of the product.

## Support should increase product intelligence

Support is not only a queue to empty.

It is a sensor for broken expectations, confusing models, missing proof, reliability problems, unsafe defaults, and opportunities to own more of the result.

Resolve the immediate issue.

Then ask whether the product, process, promise, or customer selection should change so the issue becomes less likely.

Do not turn every request into a feature. Look for repeated structure and economic weight.

## Earn retention and expansion

Retention follows repeated value, embedded workflow position, accumulated context, trust, and a responsibility the customer does not want to rebuild.

Do not use friction, data hostage, or operational dependence as a substitute for value.

Expansion should follow a proven adjacent outcome, broader authority, more volume, another actor, or a larger responsibility the company can now defend.

Do not sell expansion faster than delivery can preserve trust.

## Close the loop with Product and GTM

Post-sale reality should change who the company targets, what it promises, what proof it shows, what the product owns, and what it refuses.

Do not isolate customer success from product decisions.

Do not let pre-sale commitments silently become permanent customer obligations.

Make contradictions visible while they are still cheap to correct.

## Do not inherit

Do not assume:

- onboarding completion means activation
- usage automatically means value
- low usage automatically means churn
- support is separate from product discovery
- services make a software company less scalable
- automation should replace every human touch
- retention can compensate for a weak outcome
- expansion is always healthy growth
- the customer should coordinate internal handoffs
- every exception deserves a permanent workflow

## Human gate

Escalate changes to customer promises, authority, service obligations, data use, consequential exceptions, contractual responsibility, recovery commitments, pricing tied to delivery, or decisions that may materially harm customer trust.

Agents may improve onboarding, remove friction, automate understood work, detect risk, strengthen proof, and resolve bounded issues within granted authority.

## Done

Customer work is done when the promised change happened, the customer can recognize it, exceptions were handled, proof exists, responsibility remained clear, and the company learned enough to make the next outcome faster or more reliable.
