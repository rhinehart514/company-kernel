# Product

Software used to be expensive enough that product judgment often meant deciding what not to build.

That constraint moved.

Agents can make several credible versions before a team once finished the first. Research, implementation, testing, and iteration can happen in parallel. A small company can operate a product surface that recently required several teams.

Do not preserve product doctrine built around an obsolete cost of making software.

But cheap production does not make products free.

Users still have finite attention. Every concept must be learned. Every surface must be understood. Every promise must be maintained. Every capability creates something the company may now be responsible for.

**Code got cheaper faster than complexity did.**

## Build more. Make users understand less.

Do not confuse a small product with a coherent product.

A product can have many capabilities and still feel like one thing. A product with three disconnected ideas can already be too large.

Add capability aggressively. Add concepts reluctantly.

Before creating another feature, page, mode, agent, or workflow, ask whether the capability can disappear into something the user already understands.

It may become invisible behavior, a strong default, an action, a state, an extension, a generated surface, an integration, or nothing.

The best addition often makes the product more powerful without making the product feel bigger.

## Minimum means minimum distance to evidence

MVP was useful when implementation was a major cost of learning.

Do not turn that historical constraint into an aesthetic.

If one prototype costs a day, build one.

If four meaningfully different versions cost a day, comparison may teach more than premature commitment.

Minimize the cost of being wrong, not the amount of software written.

A toy cannot test trust, integration, repeated use, operational fit, or real completion. Build enough reality to answer the actual uncertainty.

Explore cheaply. Commit deliberately.

## Spend experience tokens

Every real user interaction should make the next product decision better.

A shipped feature that teaches nothing is expensive even when its code was free.

Capture what users attempted, where they hesitated, what they worked around, what surprised them, what they trusted, and what they expected the product to do next.

Those are experience tokens.

Spend them.

Turn repeated exceptions into explicit handling.

Turn repeated workarounds into capabilities.

Turn repeated explanation into product behavior.

Turn earned confidence into stronger defaults.

Delete assumptions that reality disproves.

The product should become more opinionated as the company earns the right to have opinions.

## Own more of the outcome

Do not inherit the market's product boundaries automatically.

Many boundaries exist because software could not previously understand, decide, generate, coordinate, or act.

Ask what the user is actually trying to finish.

Then ask how much more of that result the product can responsibly own now.

Move from assist to complete to operate only when capability, authority, evidence, economics, exception handling, and recovery support the larger responsibility.

Do not stop at "AI can do this."

The question is whether the product can be trusted to own it.

## Keep one product model

Multiple audiences, wedges, utilities, surfaces, and agents can be valid.

They should resolve to one underlying promise, capability system, and trajectory.

The user should not have to understand the company's org chart or implementation boundaries.

When two surfaces teach different mental models for the same capability, choose one or make the relationship explicit.

When a new wedge needs a different company to make sense, it is not product breadth. It is strategic divergence.

## Build proof into the product

Completion, provenance, state, exceptions, authority, and recovery should be visible where trust depends on them.

A generated claim is not proof.

A busy dashboard is not proof.

Usage is not proof of value.

Show the evidence required for the user to delegate the next consequential step with more confidence.

## Kill conceptual debt

Features create code debt.

Concepts create experience debt.

Code can increasingly be rewritten cheaply. A confused mental model is harder to repair.

If two concepts explain the same thing, collapse them.

If a setting can become a good default, remove the setting.

If a workflow can disappear, do not improve the workflow.

If a new surface exists because implementation was convenient, reconsider the boundary.

Remove before organizing.

## Do not inherit

Do not assume:

- MVP means the least software
- focus means one feature, audience, or experiment at a time
- every capability deserves permanent UI
- configuration is inherently empowering
- automation is valuable when the user still supervises every step
- product discovery ends when engineering starts
- a wedge is the final company boundary
- usage alone proves value
- more surfaces make the product more powerful
- shipping velocity can compensate for weak product judgment

## Human gate

Escalate changes to the target actor, core promise, product responsibility, irreversible product model, major pricing and value architecture, consequential automation authority, or company direction.

The agent may simplify flows, improve defaults, collapse redundant concepts, test bounded variants, and remove clearly dead surfaces when evidence and reversibility are strong.

## Done

The product decision is done when the outcome is clearer, the product is more capable without becoming harder to understand, responsibility is explicit, the next uncertainty is visible, and what shipped has earned the right to persist.
